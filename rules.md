# Resume Review Rules

These rules combine generic editorial standards derived from resume-writing
principles in the MIT Career Handbook 2019 with abstract formatting principles
from Harvard's accessible resume template. They contain no sample resumes,
names, schools, employers, or personal data.

## Structure

- Put the most relevant evidence first. Choose the section order based on the
  target role and the strongest parts of the candidate's record.
- Use a contact header with the candidate's name, email, phone, location, and
  relevant portfolio or professional link.
- Use standard section headings. Common headings include Education, Experience,
  Leadership, Projects, Research, Teaching, Publications, Awards, Skills, and
  Activities.
- List roles, degrees, projects, and other dated items in reverse chronological
  order unless a different order clearly serves the target role.
- For each experience entry, show the organization, location, role, and dates.
  Keep the ordering consistent across entries.
- Put the organization and role on one line, followed by dates on the same line
  or immediately below. Keep this pattern consistent throughout the resume.
- Education entries should include the institution, degree, graduation date,
  and relevant honors, coursework, or GPA when they add useful context.
- Write two to five focused bullets for each substantive experience. Use fewer
  bullets for short or less relevant roles.
- Use bullets for experience, research, projects, leadership, and teaching.
  Do not write these sections as narrative paragraphs.
- Group skills by category, such as programming, analysis, laboratory methods,
  design tools, languages, or domain expertise. Do not bury skills in a long
  paragraph.
- Include awards, fellowships, publications, and service when they strengthen
  the candidate's case. Add selectivity, scale, or context when known.
- Use a short summary only when it helps the reader understand the candidate's
  target role, level, and strongest evidence. Keep it to two or three sentences.
- Omit or shorten older, unrelated material when space is limited. Relevance
  matters more than completeness.

## Tone & Style

- Write in a direct, factual, professional voice.
- Prefer concrete evidence over adjectives, labels, and claims of personality.
- Describe results with numbers, scale, time, money, people, rankings, or a
  clear qualitative outcome when those facts are available.
- Use the PAR pattern when the facts allow it: Project or context, Activity,
  and Result. Do not invent a missing part.
- Start experience bullets with a specific action verb.
- Preserve the candidate's facts, meaning, names, dates, titles, employers,
  institutions, tools, awards, and publications.
- Never invent or infer a metric, responsibility, title, degree, date, tool,
  publication, or outcome. Mark missing facts for the candidate to confirm.
- Avoid first-person pronouns and unnecessary articles at the start of bullets.
- Avoid vague phrases such as "responsible for," "worked on," "helped with,"
  "various," "etc.," "team player," and "excellent communication."
- Do not describe a duty without showing the action, scope, or result that made
  it valuable.
- Use consistent verb tense: past tense for completed roles and present tense
  for current responsibilities.
- Use consistent capitalization for headings, job titles, and proper nouns.
- Keep terminology relevant to the target field and explain uncommon acronyms
  when space allows.
- Do not use emojis, decorative symbols, or conversational filler in the resume.
- Do not turn the resume into a cover letter, personal essay, or list of
  unsupported strengths.
- Translate faithfully when translation is requested. Preserve proper nouns and
  mark genuinely ambiguous wording for review instead of guessing.

## Action Verbs

Use specific, truthful verbs. Choose the verb that matches what the candidate
actually did.

- Leadership and ownership: led, directed, coordinated, managed, supervised,
  planned, organized, launched, initiated, delegated, mentored.
- Creation and design: built, created, designed, developed, produced,
  established, implemented, prototyped, authored, composed.
- Analysis and research: analyzed, investigated, evaluated, tested, measured,
  modeled, audited, compared, identified, validated, synthesized.
- Improvement and problem solving: improved, reduced, increased, streamlined,
  optimized, resolved, repaired, simplified, accelerated, eliminated.
- Communication and collaboration: presented, explained, trained, advised,
  facilitated, negotiated, collaborated, documented, translated, reported.
- Operations and delivery: delivered, executed, maintained, processed,
  scheduled, tracked, monitored, inspected, coordinated, shipped.
- Teaching and service: taught, tutored, instructed, coached, supported,
  volunteered, organized, facilitated, advised.
- Technical and quantitative work: programmed, automated, calculated,
  benchmarked, configured, debugged, deployed, integrated, quantified.

Avoid weak or vague openings unless the source text does not support a stronger
verb: responsible for, worked on, helped with, handled, participated in,
assisted with, involved in, used, utilized.

## Formatting

- Prefer one page for early-career and standard professional resumes. Two pages
  may be appropriate for advanced degrees, research records, or substantial
  relevant experience.
- Use consistent margins, font sizes, line spacing, and section spacing.
- Use a readable professional font at a consistent size. Keep body text large
  enough to scan and headings visibly distinct without excessive decoration.
- Use one consistent heading style. The same level of heading should not be
  uppercase on one page and title case on another.
- Use one date format throughout, such as Month Year to Month Year. Do not mix
  numeric and written month formats in the same resume.
- Keep bullets short, normally one or two lines. Avoid dense blocks of text.
- Leave a clear blank line or rule between major sections.
- Keep contact details in a compact, readable header. Do not scatter contact
  information through the resume.
- Left-align the main text for predictable scanning and ATS parsing.
- Avoid tables, text boxes, icons, photographs, and multi-column text in the
  plain-text or ATS version.
- Do not place critical content only in a header, footer, image, or graphic.
- Keep punctuation, bullet style, abbreviation style, and capitalization
  consistent.
- Spell-check the final document and verify every date, number, email address,
  phone number, and URL.
- Preserve a clean plain-text version when the final document is exported to
  PDF. The PDF text should remain selectable and searchable.
- Do not add Markdown notation, code fences, or commentary inside the final
  resume text.

## Formatting Rules (Harvard-inspired)

Use these as layout and accessibility rules only. They are not content to copy.

- Open with a compact contact header containing the candidate's name, location,
  email, phone, and relevant professional or portfolio link.
- For student and early-career resumes, place Education first. Include the
  institution, location, degree or concentration, graduation date, and optional
  GPA, honors, thesis, study abroad, or relevant coursework when useful.
- Choose the remaining section order by relevance. A common student order is
  Education, Experience, Leadership and Activities, then Skills and Interests.
- Move Leadership and Activities above Experience when that section is stronger
  evidence for the target role.
- Place a Technical Skills or Projects section before Experience when technical
  skills are central to the target role.
- Use conventional section labels and keep the same label for the same section
  throughout the resume.
- List entries within each section in reverse chronological order.
- Use a consistent entry pattern: organization and location, then role and
  dates, then bullets. Keep the alignment and order identical across entries.
- Use one date format and one date placement throughout the document.
- Use one professional, highly legible sans-serif font throughout. The
  reference template uses Calibri; choose an equivalent if unavailable.
- Keep body text around 10.5 to 11.5 points, make the name the largest text,
  and use slightly larger bold section headings. Do not vary font sizes without
  a clear hierarchy.
- Use 0.5 to 1 inch margins. The reference template uses 0.5-inch margins on
  all sides.
- Use approximately 1.0 to 1.15 line spacing and small, consistent spacing
  before and after section headings.
- Use real bullet-list formatting for experience, projects, leadership, and
  activities. Keep most bullets to one line and generally no more than two.
- Begin each bullet with an action verb. Write a phrase rather than a complete
  sentence, include scope and method when known, and quantify results when the
  facts support it.
- Use about three to five bullets for a substantive entry and fewer for short
  or less relevant entries.
- Group skills by meaningful category, such as programming, design tools,
  languages, laboratory methods, or domain expertise. State proficiency only
  when it is accurate.
- Keep a simple, single-column reading order. Use selectable text, built-in
  heading styles, real list formatting, and descriptive link text so the file
  remains accessible to screen readers and applicant tracking systems.
- Keep the resume to one page for student and early-career candidates. Use two
  pages only when relevant research, publications, or experience justify it.
- Keep the layout readable in black and white and verify that spacing remains
  consistent after exporting to PDF.
- Keep contact details and important content in the main document body, not
  only in a header, footer, text box, or image.
- Maintain consistent capitalization, punctuation, abbreviations, indentation,
  and spacing across every section.

Avoid:

- Personal pronouns and full-sentence bullets.
- Paragraphs where a short bullet list would be easier to scan.
- Decorative fonts, tiny type, icons, photographs, tables, text boxes,
  multi-column layouts, or graphics that interfere with accessibility or ATS
  parsing.
- Inconsistent heading styles, date formats, margins, bullet indentation, or
  section spacing.
- Unsupported adjectives, vague claims, and bullets that list duties without an
  action, scope, or result.
- Irrelevant or outdated content that displaces stronger evidence.
- Critical information placed only inside an image, header, footer, or link.
